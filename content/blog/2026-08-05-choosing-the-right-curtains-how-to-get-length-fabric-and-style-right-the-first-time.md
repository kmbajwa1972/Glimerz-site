Curtains are one of the largest surfaces in any room, and yet they're often the last thing anyone thinks about — an afterthought bought in a rush, in whatever length happened to be in stock. That's a shame, because few single purchases affect a room's feel as much as the curtains hanging in it. Too short and a room looks unfinished; the wrong fabric and it either blocks light you wanted or lets in light you didn't. Here's how to get curtains right the first time, without the trial and error.

**Length: The Single Biggest Mistake People Make**

If there's one rule to remember from this whole guide, it's this: curtains that stop a few inches above the floor make a room look cheap and unfinished, no matter how nice the fabric is. The three acceptable lengths are floor-skimming (just barely grazing the floor), puddle (a few inches of extra fabric pooling on the floor, elegant in formal rooms), or hovering exactly half an inch above the floor. Anything that stops mid-window or awkwardly at sill height reads as a mistake, not a style choice — unless you're deliberately doing cafe curtains on a kitchen window, which is its own specific look. Measure from where your rod sits down to the floor, not from the top of the window frame, since the rod should be mounted higher than the frame anyway (more on that below).

**Width: Fuller Always Looks Better**

The second most common mistake is buying curtains that are too narrow, so they hang flat and skimpy even when closed. As a rule, each curtain panel should be roughly one and a half to two times the width of the window itself, so that when drawn, the fabric falls in soft folds rather than pulling tight like a bedsheet. This means most people need to buy two panels per window, not one, and often need to size up from what a single width-of-window calculation suggests. Fuller curtains simply look more expensive, full stop.

**Mount Rods High and Wide**

Here's a trick that costs nothing extra but transforms how a window looks: mount your curtain rod several inches above the actual window frame, and extend it several inches beyond each side of the window. This makes the window look taller and grander than it is, and when curtains are open, they clear the glass entirely instead of blocking part of it and stealing natural light. A rod mounted directly on the window frame, hugging it tightly, is the look of a rental unit rushing to check a box — a few extra inches of rod on each side is the look of someone who actually planned the room.

**Choosing Fabric for the Room's Job**

Different rooms need curtains to do different jobs, and fabric weight is how you control that. Living rooms and dining rooms usually want medium-weight fabrics like linen or cotton blends that let in soft, diffused light while still offering privacy in the evening — these fabrics also drape beautifully in fuller folds. Bedrooms benefit from heavier fabric or a blackout lining behind a decorative outer layer, since darkness matters more for sleep than for anywhere else in the home. Kitchens and bathrooms do best with lighter, easy-care fabrics that won't trap cooking odors or bathroom humidity — cotton or linen blends that can be machine washed win here over anything delicate.

**Sheers: Not Just for Layering**

Sheer curtains get treated as an optional extra, but they solve a real problem: how to get privacy and soft, diffused light during the day without blocking the view or making a room feel closed off. Hung alone, sheers work beautifully in a room that doesn't face direct street-level foot traffic. Layered behind a heavier curtain, they give you the best of both worlds — sheers closed during the day for gentle privacy and light, heavier panels drawn at night for full coverage. If you've never used sheers, a north- or east-facing living room window is the easiest place to start.

**Blackout Linings: Where They Actually Matter**

Full blackout curtains aren't necessary everywhere, but in certain rooms they change everything. Bedrooms, especially ones facing streetlights, sunrise-side windows, or shift workers who sleep during the day, benefit enormously from genuine blackout lining rather than just "room-darkening" fabric, which is a lighter, less effective compromise. A home theater or media room is the other obvious candidate. Everywhere else — living rooms, kitchens, home offices — blackout lining tends to make a room feel heavier and more closed off than it needs to be, so save it for the rooms where darkness is actually the goal.

**Color and Pattern: Let the Room Guide You**

Curtains in a color close to your wall color make a room feel larger, since the boundary between wall and window blurs and the eye keeps traveling. Curtains in a contrasting or bolder color make a deliberate style statement and can act as the room's main accent color — useful if your walls and furniture are neutral and you want one confident splash of personality. Patterned curtains work best when nothing else large in the room competes with them; if your sofa or rug already carries a pattern, keep the curtains solid so the room doesn't compete with itself.

**Hardware: The Detail Everyone Forgets**

A stunning curtain fabric can be undercut entirely by a flimsy plastic rod or mismatched rings. Curtain rings should glide smoothly and match your rod's finish — matte black, brushed brass, and satin nickel are the current go-to finishes that work with almost any room. Finials, the decorative ends on a rod, are a small detail but a noticeable one; a plain rod without them can look unfinished the same way a room without baseboards does. If your budget allows only one splurge in this whole project, a genuinely sturdy rod with a finish you love is usually worth it over the fabric itself, since it's what your eye actually registers at a glance.

**The Takeaway**

Great curtains come down to a short list of decisions made correctly: length that reaches the floor, width generous enough to fall in folds, a rod mounted high and wide, and fabric matched to what the room actually needs — light control, privacy, or both. Get those right, and even simple, inexpensive curtains will look considered and pulled-together. Get them wrong, and no amount of fabric quality will save the room from looking unfinished.
