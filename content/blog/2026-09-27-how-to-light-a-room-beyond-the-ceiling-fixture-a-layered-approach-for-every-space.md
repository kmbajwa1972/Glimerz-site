 **How to Light a Room Beyond the Ceiling Fixture**

Most rooms come with one ceiling light and most of us just leave it like that. The room looks okay but it never feels cozy, because all the light comes from above, the corners stay dark and there are shadows under the furniture. You don't need expensive fixtures to fix this. You just need a few more light sources placed in the right spots. This guide is for living rooms, bedrooms, dining rooms and hallways, and if you want the kitchen version, we covered that in our [kitchen lighting guide](/blog/2026-07-17-kitchen-lighting-done-right-the-three-layer-approach-designers-swear-by/).

**The three kinds of light every room needs**

A good room has general light so you can see the whole space, task light where you read or work, and accent light that adds warmth and depth. General light doesn't have to come from the ceiling, because a big floor lamp in the corner can do the same job and it looks much softer. Task light is whatever sits close to what you're doing, like a reading lamp by the bed or a lamp on your desk. Accent light is the one people skip, things like a small lamp on a shelf, LED strips in a bookcase or a light behind a plant. Take it away and the room feels empty even if you can't say why. You don't need all three in every corner, but every room you spend your evenings in should have at least one of each.

**Getting the height right**

For a table lamp next to a sofa, the table and lamp together should be about 58 to 64 inches tall, so the bottom of the shade sits near your eye level when you're sitting. If you can see the bulb from the sofa, it's too high. Floor lamps that point up work well in rooms with normal ceilings because the light bounces off the ceiling and spreads softly, and arc lamps are great over a reading chair. Don't hide a floor lamp in a crowded corner, since it works better pulled a little into the room. Wall sconces usually go about 60 inches from the floor, a bit lower in a hallway and a bit higher in a dining room.

**Choosing the right chandelier**

The chandelier is the one light everyone notices, so it's worth getting the size right. A simple rule for the room is to add the length and width of the room in feet, and use that number in inches as the chandelier width. So a 12 by 14 foot dining room works well with a chandelier around 26 inches wide. Over a table, the chandelier should be about half to two-thirds the width of the table, so it never hangs past the edges where people could bump their heads when they stand up. For a long rectangular table, a linear chandelier usually looks better than a round one, because it spreads light along the whole table instead of making one bright spot in the middle. Try to leave at least 6 inches between the ends of the fixture and the ends of the table. Style matters too. A drum or farmhouse chandelier suits a relaxed, cozy room, a simple black or brass linear one looks clean in a modern space, and glass or crystal works when you want the room to feel a little dressy. Whatever you pick, make sure it's dimmable. Most chandeliers use small candelabra bulbs (E12 base), so buy dimmable LED ones in warm white, and check they work with your dimmer switch, because cheap bulbs often flicker or buzz on a dimmer.

**Dining room**

A chandelier or pendant over the dining table on its own makes a spotlight in the middle and leaves the edges dark, so add two dimmable sconces on the long wall. Hang the chandelier 30 to 34 inches above the table if your ceiling is 8 feet, and add about 3 inches for every extra foot. If the chandelier isn't over a table, like in an entry or an open living area, the bottom should be at least 7 feet from the floor so nobody walks into it. Pick sconces in the same finish as the chandelier so they look like a set, or if you're mixing brass and black finishes, our [mixing metals guide](/blog/2026-08-09-mixing-metals-in-your-home-the-simple-rules-that-make-it-actually-work/) will help. Put everything on dimmers and you can go from bright family dinner to soft light for guests with one switch. If you're adding hardwired sconces or swapping a fixture and you're not comfortable with wiring, get an electrician, and always turn the breaker off first.

**Bedroom**

In the bedroom you need bright light in the morning and soft light at night. Plug-in wall sconces about 24 to 28 inches above the mattress are better than table lamps if your nightstand is small, and they keep the surface free for your phone and a glass of water. Point reading lights down at the page, not out into the room, so the other person can sleep. A small lamp on the dresser, even a rechargeable one on a timer, stops the room from feeling like a plain hotel room. Keep bedroom bulbs warm, because bright cool light late at night makes it harder to wind down.

**Living room**

Try to have at least three lights you can switch on separately, like a floor lamp, a table lamp and one accent light such as LED strips behind the TV. If you only have one ceiling light, put a dimmable smart bulb in it and add two lamps on opposite sides of the sofa. A [console table](/blog/2026-09-05-how-to-style-a-console-table-10-simple-home-decor-ideas-that-look-designer/) behind the sofa is an easy spot for one of them. Use warm white bulbs around 2700K to 3000K, and keep all bulbs in the room the same color so nothing looks cold or patchy. For brightness, a rough guide for a living room is 10 to 20 lumens per square foot in total, so a 200 square foot room needs about 2,000 to 4,000 lumens spread across all its lights, not all from one fixture.

**Hallways and entry**

Hallways are usually the darkest part of the house and the easiest to fix. In a long hallway, one ceiling light every 8 to 10 feet keeps it evenly lit without dark patches. If you can't add ceiling lights, plug-in or rechargeable wall sconces at around 56 to 58 inches work well, and a motion sensor night light near the floor is great for late night trips to the kitchen. In the entry, a small chandelier or a lamp on a console table makes the first thing people see feel warm and welcoming.

**Common mistakes to avoid**

The biggest one is buying a fixture that's too small, especially over a dining table where a tiny pendant looks lost. The second is mixing bulb colors, like a warm lamp next to a cool white ceiling light, which makes the whole room look cheap. People also put every light on one switch, so they can only have everything on or everything off. And lampshades matter more than you think, because a dark or thick shade can make a good lamp almost useless for reading.

**Where to start**

Count the lights in the room you use most. If there's only one, or they all turn on together, that's why the room feels flat. Add one lamp to the darkest corner, match your bulb colors and dim the ceiling light. That usually costs under $100 and makes a bigger difference than buying new furniture. When you're ready for a bigger change, start with the dining room chandelier, since it's the light your guests will notice first.
